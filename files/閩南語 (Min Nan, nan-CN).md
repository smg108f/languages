Hokkien is the language of sailors and shopkeepers who left home centuries ago and never quite came back, scattering from a handful of Fujian port cities into Chinatowns and night markets from Manila to Penang to San Francisco. It never settled on a single way to write itself down, so a missionary romanization and a patchwork of borrowed Chinese characters coexist without much apology, punctuates its temple festivals with opera troupes and firecrackers loud enough to wake the gods, and considers a bowl of braised pork rice a more reliable marker of home than any actual address.

#### eras
---
Min Nan changes with whoever's controlling the Taiwan Strait and how far its sailors and traders have scattered: an independent maritime kingdom that gave the language its very name, a Ming loyalist regime that turned an island into the last holdout against a new dynasty, a colonial administration that modernized the island under a foreign flag, a retrocession that reopened old wounds, and now a globally dispersed diaspora broadcasting Hokkien pop and night-market food to Southeast Asia and beyond.
