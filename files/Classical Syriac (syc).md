Classical Syriac is the literary register of Aramaic that a minor buffer kingdom on the Roman-Persian frontier turned into one of Christianity's great scholarly languages, more or less by accident of geography: the Kingdom of Osroene was founded around 132 BCE with Edessa as its capital, and by 200 CE its King Abgar VIII had reportedly embraced Christianity at court, giving the city a head start on becoming a Christian intellectual capital nobody had originally planned for. Its literary golden age arrived when the Persians captured Nisibis in 363 CE and its resident theologian, Ephrem the Syrian, decamped to Edessa and got to work on the hymns that would later fill the Beth Gazo, the roughly 700-melody chant repository still sung by Syriac Orthodox choirs today. Its monks got their comportment regulated in unusually granular detail once Rabbula became Bishop of Edessa around 412 CE and issued a set of Admonitions governing exactly how ascetics were meant to carry themselves in silence. Its unity didn't survive the fifth century's theological arguments: the Council of Ephesus in 431 split East Syriac Christianity toward Persia and the Council of Chalcedon in 451 pushed West Syriac Christians the other direction, and by 489 the Byzantine emperor Zeno had simply closed the School of Edessa outright, sending its scholars east to found a rival school at Nisibis instead. Its speakers, whichever side of the schism they landed on, spent the following centuries getting drafted into the Abbasid Caliphate's translation projects, rendering Greek philosophy and medicine into Arabic by way of Syriac intermediaries. Its community was nearly wiped out in the Sayfo of 1914-1920, when an estimated half a million to three-quarters of a million Syriac, Assyrian, and Chaldean Christians were killed across the late Ottoman Empire (disputed by source), scattering survivors into diaspora cities like Södertälje and Chicago and Fairfield, where the language now survives almost entirely as a liturgical and academic pursuit.

#### eras
---
Classical Syriac changes with whoever's ruling Edessa or arguing theology: an Osroene/Early Edessan Era once the Abgarid kings make Edessa their capital, a Christian Edessa Era once Abgar VIII's court embraces Christianity, a Classical Golden Age Era once Ephrem the Syrian relocates to Edessa and its literary output peaks, a Denominational Schism Era once the Councils of Ephesus and Chalcedon split East and West Syriac Christianity from each other, and a Modern Diaspora/Liturgical-Only Era once the Sayfo genocide scatters its speakers worldwide.
