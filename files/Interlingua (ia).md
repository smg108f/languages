Interlingua is the constructed language built not by a single inspired inventor but by a well-funded committee, the product of decades of patient linguistic research bankrolled by American heiress Alice Vanderbilt Morris and her diplomat husband Dave Hennen Morris, who founded the International Auxiliary Language Association in New York City on May 15, 1924, to figure out scientifically what an international language should actually look like. Rather than inventing new vocabulary from scratch the way Volapük or Esperanto had, IALA's linguists spent from 1937 onward extracting the vocabulary and grammar naturally shared across English, French, Italian, Spanish, and Portuguese, reasoning that a language built from the common ancestry those tongues already share would be instantly readable to hundreds of millions of people without anyone needing to learn it first. Under linguist Alexander Gode's direction, that research finally crystallized into the Interlingua-English Dictionary, published in 1951, the language's foundational text and the first place its full vocabulary appeared in coherent form. IALA itself formally dissolved not long after, its work essentially complete, but Interlingua's speaker community regrouped in 1955 as the Union Mundial pro Interlingua, which still organizes a biennial international conference and continues publishing the periodical Panorama in Interlingua to this day. Because Interlingua has no dedicated gestural tradition of its own, it borrows one from the country where it was born: American Sign Language traces its own institutional founding to 1817, when Thomas Hopkins Gallaudet and Laurent Clerc opened the American School for the Deaf in Hartford, Connecticut, the first permanent school for deaf students in the United States and the seedbed from which ASL as it's known today grew.
