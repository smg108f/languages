Glosa began as one scientist's wartime insomnia project and only became a language for the rest of the world decades later, under different management. British biologist Lancelot Hogben devised Interglossa during the empty hours of fire-watching duty in Aberdeen, Scotland during the Second World War, publishing it in 1943 as Interglossa: A Draft of an Auxiliary for a Democratic World Order, a roughly 880-word isolating-grammar language built almost entirely from international Greek and Latin scientific roots. The project sat largely dormant until 1960, when Ron Clark came across Hogben's book, sought the professor out, and began working with him to refine and expand it; Wendy Ashby joined the effort in 1972. When Hogben died in 1975, Clark and Ashby carried the project forward, renaming it Glosa and shifting to a simplified phonetic spelling less tied to Greek orthographic conventions. The first Glosa dictionary appeared in 1978, and in 1987 Clark and Ashby co-founded the Glosa Education Organisation (GEO), a UK-registered charity based in Richmond, to promote the teaching of Glosa in schools worldwide. Because Glosa has no gestural tradition of its own, and Interglossa itself was born in Scotland, it borrows a thematic analog from that country's deaf-rights history: the British Sign Language (Scotland) Act, which received royal assent on October 22, 2015.
