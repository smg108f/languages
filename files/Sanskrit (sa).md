Sanskrit is the language whose oldest hymns were composed and memorized centuries before anyone wrote them down, transmitted so precisely from teacher to student across a thousand years of pure recitation that scholars still trust the oral tradition over most surviving manuscripts. It didn't get a systematic grammar until around 500 BCE, when a scholar named Panini laid out nearly 4,000 rules covering the entire language in a treatise so complete it's still taught as a linguistic model today. Its oldest known long inscription wasn't carved until roughly 150 CE, on a rock face in Gujarat commissioned by a regional ruler who chose Sanskrit prose specifically to distinguish his monument from the plainer Prakrit that the emperor Ashoka had used four centuries earlier. A treatise on dramatic performance, compiled sometime between 200 BCE and 200 CE, codified 37 distinct hand positions actors could use to convey meaning without a word of dialogue, a gestural vocabulary still taught in Indian classical dance today. Its most famous seat of learning was burned for months in 1193 CE when an invading army torched a library said to hold nine million manuscripts. It still gets chanted in temple ritual, still gets used to name mathematical constants and yoga poses, and still gets spoken as a first language in at least one Karnataka village that decided, decades ago, to simply keep using it.

#### eras
---
Sanskrit changes with whoever's compiling its texts: a Vedic Sanskrit era of orally transmitted hymns, a Classical Sanskrit era once Panini standardizes its grammar, an Epic/Puranic Sanskrit era once the great epics take shape, a Golden Age Sanskrit era under Gupta imperial patronage, and a Modern Revival era once Western scholarship rediscovers it.
