Ancient Greek is the language whose earliest attested form was written not in the familiar 24-letter alphabet but in a syllabic script scratched onto clay palace tablets around 1450 BCE, a system so thoroughly forgotten that scholars didn't crack it until 1952. It didn't get the alphabet historians actually associate with it until Greek merchants borrowed and adapted the Phoenicians' letters sometime around 750 BCE, finally giving Homer's oral epics something to be written down in. Its dominant city-state didn't become the democracy everyone remembers until 508 BCE, when a reformer named Cleisthenes broke the old aristocratic tribes into new ones specifically to dilute regional power blocs. Its speakers fought each other into exhaustion during a 27-year war that killed off a third to two-thirds of Athens's population through plague alone, and then watched their independence end for good in 338 BCE when Philip of Macedon's army settled the matter at Chaeronea. One of its own philosophers, arguing in a dialogue about how words get their meanings, casually used deaf people's gestural communication as a debating point around 385 BCE, in what's now read as the oldest surviving Western reference to sign language. It still gets recited at classics conferences, still gets carved onto university seals, and still gets blamed, correctly, for roughly a tenth of the English dictionary.

#### eras
---
Ancient Greek changes with whoever's dominating the Aegean: a Mycenaean Greek era of Linear B palace records, an Archaic/Homeric Greek era of oral epic and the new alphabet, a Classical Greek era once Athenian democracy takes hold, a Hellenistic/Koine Greek era once Alexander's conquests spread it eastward, and a Byzantine Greek era once Constantinople becomes its capital.
