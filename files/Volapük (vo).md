Volapük is the language that briefly convinced 19th-century Europe that a single artificial tongue might actually unite humanity, all because a Catholic priest in a small German parish couldn't sleep one night. Johann Martin Schleyer, pastor of Litzelstetten near Konstanz in the Grand Duchy of Baden, suffered a severe bout of insomnia on the night of March 31, 1879, during which he became convinced God wanted him to build an international auxiliary language, and by morning he'd sketched out its grammar; he published the first outline that May in Sionsharfe, the Catholic magazine he edited, and followed it with a full textbook in 1880. The name itself is a compressed mashup of English "world" and "speak" filtered through Schleyer's idiosyncratic phonology, which shaved consonant clusters down to something he considered universally pronounceable, even if the results looked nothing like the source languages anymore. Volapük exploded across Europe astonishingly fast: by its 1889 Paris congress, the movement claimed roughly 283 clubs, 25 periodicals devoted to it, and 316 textbooks translated into 25 languages, a scale of enthusiasm no constructed language would match again until Esperanto came along. The bubble popped almost as quickly, though, when reformers in the language's own International Academy clashed with Schleyer over his refusal to loosen his personal proprietary control, and when the Academy defected to rival projects, Schleyer founded a smaller, loyalist "Volapük Academy" back home in Konstanz in 1890 to keep his original vision alive. Volapük never really recovered its 1880s momentum, but it did get one absurd second act: in 2007, a Volapük enthusiast built a bot that auto-generated articles on the Volapük Wikipedia, briefly ballooning it past 100,000 entries, most of them one-line stubs about towns nobody involved could actually read about in the language supposedly describing them.
