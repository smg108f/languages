Kotava is what happens when a constructed-language designer decides that even Esperanto is too culturally biased. Staren Fetcey, a Canadian traveler and linguist, began the project in the summer of 1975 after studying earlier international auxiliary language projects and concluding that most of them, Esperanto included, still leaned too heavily on a Western linguistic substrate; she set out instead to build Kotava's roots entirely from scratch, deliberately independent of any existing language or culture. Fetcey first made the language public in 1978, and it underwent two major revisions, in 1988 and 1993, as its vocabulary grew past 17,000 basic roots. In 2005, the international Comité Linguistique Kotava (Kotava Avaneda), a seven-member committee, was established to guide the language's ongoing evolution and resolve disputes over new vocabulary, and the project, though known mainly among French-speaking constructed-language enthusiasts, has since produced translations including a rendition of Le Petit Prince. Kotava reached its most visible modern milestone on July 29, 2020, when a Kotava-language edition of Wikipedia launched, growing to nearly 30,000 articles within five years. Because Kotava has no gestural tradition of its own, and its creator is Canadian, it borrows a thematic analog from that country's deaf-rights history: the Accessible Canada Act, which received royal assent on June 21, 2019 and formally recognized American Sign Language, Quebec Sign Language, and Indigenous sign languages.
