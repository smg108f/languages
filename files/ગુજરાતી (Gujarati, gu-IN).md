Gujarati is the language of a trading-and-textile culture so committed to the practical over the ornamental that it dropped the decorative headline stroke from its parent alphabet just to write ledgers faster, and it repaid the favor by producing an autobiography one of its native sons wrote almost entirely about his own moral failings, which then helped free an entire subcontinent. It reorganized its own state along linguistic lines rather than keep sharing one with a neighboring language, treats a nine-night circle dance as a competitive-sized public event that regularly sets crowd-size world records, and exported an entire demographic of hoteliers who, by some counts, now own close to half of America's small-town motels. It considers a sweet-savory steamed snack and a many-vegetable winter stew basic food groups in their own right, flies kites competitively enough to give an entire city an annual public holiday, and turned a 15th-century devotional poet's five-minute hymn into the favorite song of the man who ended imperial rule over the subcontinent, then unofficially soundtracked his funeral.

#### eras
---
Gujarati changes with whoever's controlling the western coast: a Chaulukya-Solanki era that gave the region its first enduring capital, a Gujarat Sultanate era of independent Muslim rule, a Mughal era after Akbar's conquest, a British Raj era of colonial administration, and a post-independence statehood era born when linguistic majorities split Bombay State in two.
