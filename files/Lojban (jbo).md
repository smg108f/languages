Lojban began life as a rebellion against a trademark. James Cooke Brown had spent decades directing Loglan, his logical-language experiment for testing the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, through The Loglan Institute in Gainesville, Florida, but by the mid-1980s a group of enthusiasts led by Bob LeChevalier had grown frustrated with Brown's tight copyright and trademark control over the published grammar. In 1987 that group split off entirely, forming the Logical Language Group (LLG) and rebuilding the language from Loglan's public research so it could be developed and shared without the original institute's restrictions, renaming their fork Lojban. LLG incorporated the following year and was headquartered in Fairfax, Virginia, eventually earning IRS non-profit educational and scientific status in 1989. A decade of grammar-formalization work culminated in 1997, when LLG declared the language's design complete at its "baseline" and published the result as The Complete Lojban Language; since 2002 the community has been free to coin new words and idioms on its own, governing the language's growth collectively rather than through a single institute. Lojban's biggest jolt of public notoriety came from an unlikely source: the webcomic xkcd's 191th strip, published November 30, 2006, joked about learning Lojban for unambiguous communication only to end up talking exclusively with "the kind of people who learn Lojban" — a line so resonant within the community that it spawned its own Lojban slang term, {xekce}, for arguments about Lojban grammar that spiral out of hand. Because Lojban itself has no dedicated gestural tradition of its own, and because its predecessor Loglan already claims Gallaudet University's 1864 charter as its thematic signed-form analog, Lojban instead borrows from a distinct chapter of American deaf history: the National Association of the Deaf, founded by Deaf leaders in Cincinnati, Ohio, on August 25, 1880.
