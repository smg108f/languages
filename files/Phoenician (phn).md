Phoenician is the Northwest Semitic language of the seafaring city-states that turned an alphabet into the ancient world's most successful export, even if the Phoenicians themselves never bothered writing much history down to brag about it. Spoken across Tyre, Sidon, Byblos, and a scatter of independent city-states that never quite consolidated into a single kingdom, Phoenician's greatest historical flex is the Ahiram sarcophagus inscription, a royal curse carved around 1000 BCE that stands as the earliest known example of the fully developed 22-letter Phoenician alphabet, the direct ancestor of Greek, Latin, and by extension nearly every alphabet in use today. Tyre itself became the dominant Phoenician city-state under King Hiram I, whose reign beginning in 969 BCE and famous alliance with Solomon of Israel cemented Tyrian primacy over rival Sidon and Byblos. Phoenician religious life left behind a durable gestural tradition too: hundreds of votive stelae from sites like Umm al-Amad show dedicants, often explicitly labeled as priests, raising a single hand in the orans gesture of adoration, a wordless posture of reverence toward the gods that outlasted plenty of the prayers said alongside it. The language's most consequential colony, Carthage, grew from a Tyrian settlement into a Mediterranean superpower whose language, Punic, kept Phoenician alive centuries after the homeland cities fell into other empires' hands, until Rome burned Carthage to the ground in 146 BCE and ended organized Punic civilization for good. Phoenician itself then faded out of daily use over the following centuries, undocumented and unremembered until French scholar Jean-Jacques Barthélemy deciphered the alphabet in 1758, reopening a language that had spent two thousand years being read by absolutely nobody.

#### eras
---
