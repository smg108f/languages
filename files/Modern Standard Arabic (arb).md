Modern Standard Arabic is the language nobody grows up speaking, a deliberately preserved descendant of a seventh-century poetic and scriptural standard that twenty-two countries have agreed to use for news broadcasts, sermons, and official paperwork while actually talking to each other in a few dozen mutually semi-intelligible dialects. It insists on a right-to-left script elegant enough to be considered a devotional art form in its own right, spread across three continents behind armies that moved faster than anyone expected and a trade network that moved slower but further, and maintains a formal register so removed from the street that reciting it fluently and unscripted is treated as a genuine, separately-taught skill even by people who have spoken Arabic their entire lives.

#### eras
---
Arabic changes with whichever empire, caliphate, or satellite network is broadcasting it furthest: a pre-Islamic oral poetic tradition later canonized as literary perfection, a Quranic standard that rode a century of conquest from Arabia to Iberia, an Abbasid golden age that translated and preserved the ancient world's science in Baghdad, an Ottoman era that kept Arabic as scripture and scholarship while Turkish ran the empire, a 19th-century literary revival that modernized the language for newspapers and novels, and now a satellite-television era broadcasting a single formal standard to a billion screens speaking a dozen different Arabics at home.

#### signed
---
signed-name :: Unified Arabic Sign Language (dictionary compiled from Jordanian, Egyptian, and Saudi sign languages); largely superseded in practice by distinct national sign languages

global-signers :: 11M+ (regional disabling-hearing-loss estimate, out of the Middle East's 350M population)

signer-locations :: pan-Arab world; national sign languages (Jordanian, Egyptian, Saudi, Emirati, and others) remain the primary community languages

governing-bodies ::
Council of Arab Ministers of Social Affairs (CAMSA), which published the first Unified Arabic Sign Language dictionary in 2001; national deaf associations largely reject the unified standard in favor of their own sign languages
