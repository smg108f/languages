Interlingue, originally called Occidental, is the constructed language built by a Baltic German naval officer and schoolteacher who spent over a decade quietly perfecting it before ever showing anyone. Edgar de Wahl, based in Tallinn, Estonia, had experimented privately with an auxiliary language he called Auli from 1906 onward, but he only went public in 1922, announcing Occidental in the first issue of his new magazine Kosmoglott, published from his home city. The language's signature feature, "de Wahl's Rule," let learners derive related word forms with mechanical regularity while still looking recognizably Romance and Germanic at a glance, a naturalistic design philosophy that won it real traction among European auxiliary-language enthusiasts through the 1920s, including a notable gathering of supporters in Vienna in 1927. The movement organized institutionally in 1929 with the founding of the Interlingue-Union, which set up its lasting administrative base in Switzerland, and the language itself got a new name in 1949, rebranded Interlingue partly to signal neutrality to Cold War-era Soviet authorities and partly in hopes of merging with the rival, newly-emerging Interlingua project out of New York. That merger never happened, and Interlingue spent the 1970s through early 1990s teetering near extinction, with its flagship magazine Cosmoglotta publishing barely once a year and fewer than 100 active speakers left worldwide by some estimates. The internet gave it one more chance: digitized texts, personal websites, and small online forums pulled together a modest revival community in the 1990s that persists today in places like Reddit and Facebook groups, sustaining Interlingue as a permanent fixture of the conlang hobbyist world even without ever regaining its interwar momentum.
