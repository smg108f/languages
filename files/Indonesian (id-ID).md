Indonesian is the language of an archipelago so linguistically fractured that its 20th-century independence movement solved the problem by adopting, by youth congress vote, a variety of a neighboring country's trade language rather than any of its own thousands of native tongues, and then declaring it a brand-new national identity by simple decree. It descends from a maritime empire's inscription carved onto stone in the 7th century, survived a Dutch colonial spelling system named after a single Dutch professor, and only got its own indigenous sign language after decades of oral-method deaf schools quietly failed to stop children from signing to each other in the dormitories anyway. It still eats a coconut-and-chili rice dish that international polls keep ranking among the world's best, considers a dry cracker-topped instant noodle brand practically a national food group, and spent 1965 and 1966 in a mass killing whose true death toll, historians agree, may never be fully known.

#### eras
---
Indonesian changes with whoever's uniting the archipelago: an Old Malay/Srivijaya era shared with the wider Malay-speaking trade world, a colonial era of Dutch East Indies administration and standardized Latin spelling, a Japanese Occupation era during WWII, a National Revolution era launched by the 1945 independence proclamation, and a post-1949 sovereign era of continued nation-building.
