Láadan was built as a linguistic experiment before it was ever a novel. Suzette Haden Elgin, who earned her Ph.D. in linguistics at the University of California, San Diego, between 1970 and 1973 and later taught at San Diego State University, created the language in 1982 to test a specific version of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis: whether a language designed specifically to express women's perceptions, in ways Elgin argued existing natural languages failed to do, could change the culture of the people who spoke it. By then Elgin had retired from academia and moved to northwestern Arkansas, where she had founded the Ozark Center for Language Studies in 1980 to bring linguistics to a wider public and to work on reducing violence through language. She embedded Láadan in a work of fiction to popularize it, publishing Native Tongue in 1984, a dystopian science-fiction novel imagining a future where the Nineteenth Amendment has been repealed and women covertly build a language of their own; she followed it with two sequels, The Judas Rose in 1987 and Earthsong in 1994, completing the trilogy that carried the language into wider readership. In March 1985, the Society for the Furtherance and Study of Fantasy and Science Fiction published A First Dictionary and Grammar of Láadan, giving the language its first formal reference work outside the novels. Elgin died on January 27, 2015, after several years of declining health. Because Láadan itself has no gestural tradition, and Elgin built her later career and home in Arkansas, it borrows a thematic analog from that state's deaf history: the Arkansas School for the Deaf, established as a state institution in Little Rock on July 17, 1868.
