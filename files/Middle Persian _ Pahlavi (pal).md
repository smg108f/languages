Middle Persian, better known by its script-derived name Pahlavi, is the imperial language of the Sasanian Empire, the dynasty that spent four centuries positioning itself as Rome's only real peer in the ancient world. Its political life begins in 226 CE, when Ardashir I, having already crushed the last Parthian king two years earlier, was crowned at Ctesiphon and turned the old Parthian capital into the seat of an empire that would outlast most of its rivals. Its earliest substantial inscription belongs to Ardashir's son: the Res Gestae Divi Saporis, a triumphant trilingual account Shapur I had carved into the Ka'ba-ye Zartosht at Naqsh-e Rustam around 262 CE, boasting about his victories over Rome in Middle Persian, Parthian, and Greek all at once, presumably so nobody could claim they hadn't understood. Its everyday religious life left behind smaller, quieter traces too, like the Sasanian-period custom of holding the barsom, a bundle of sacred twigs, before saying grace at meals, a habit widely enough attested that Zoroastrian ritual manuals still describe it. Its empire didn't survive contact with the newly unified Arab armies of the 7th century: Sasanian resistance effectively ended at the Battle of Nahavand in 642 CE, after which the last shah, Yazdegerd III, spent years fleeing eastward before his death, and Zoroastrianism itself became a minority faith in its own homeland. Its scholarly tradition kept going regardless, mostly out of stubbornness: the Zoroastrian priest Adurfarnbag i Farroxzadan began compiling the Denkard, an enormous encyclopedic defense of the faith, around the mid-9th century, and a later priest, Adurbad i Emedan, was still finalizing the text in Baghdad around 1020 CE, working in Middle Persian in a city that had by then been the capital of the Abbasid Caliphate for over two and a half centuries. Its remaining religious communities today, concentrated around Yazd and Kerman in Iran and among the Parsis of Mumbai, still use fragments of Pahlavi liturgically, chanting texts whose grammar nobody has spoken naturally in well over a thousand years.

#### eras
---
Middle Persian changes with whoever's ruling from Ctesiphon or defending Zoroastrianism afterward: a Sasanian Founding Era once Ardashir I is crowned at Ctesiphon, an Imperial Golden Age Era once Shapur I inscribes his triumphs at Naqsh-e Rustam, an Arab Conquest/Islamic Transition Era once organized Sasanian resistance collapses at Nahavand, a Zoroastrian Literary Preservation Era once priests begin compiling the Denkard, and a Modern Liturgical-Only Era once that compilation is finalized far from any Zoroastrian court.
