Novial is the constructed language built by a linguist who'd already helped build a different one and decided he could still do better. Danish scholar Otto Jespersen, who became professor of English at Copenhagen University in 1893 and spent the following decades becoming one of the era's most respected authorities on English grammar and general linguistics, had been an early supporter of Ido, the reformed successor to Esperanto, before concluding it still wasn't quite right. In 1928 he introduced his own alternative in a book titled An International Language, naming it Novial, a blend of "Nov" (new) and "IAL" (International Auxiliary Language), and drawing its vocabulary primarily from Romance and Germanic sources while giving it an analytic grammar modeled loosely on English. Jespersen expanded the project in 1930 with the Novial Lexike, a dictionary that grew the language's roots to roughly 5,000 entries with equivalents across English, French, German, Spanish, and Italian, and he kept proposing refinements through the rest of the decade. The language never really outlived its creator: when Jespersen died on April 30, 1943, in Roskilde, Denmark, Novial had no comparable champion left to carry it forward, and it slid into dormancy for decades. Like so many of its constructed-language peers, Novial got an unexpected second wind once the internet arrived, with enthusiasts in the 1990s rediscovering Jespersen's grammar and dictionary and building small but persistent online communities dedicated to keeping his "new IAL" alive.
