Avestan is the language a prophet named Zoroaster composed seventeen dense hymns in, sometime around 1000 BCE by scholarly guesswork, none of it written down for over a thousand years because priests simply memorized and recited the whole thing generation after generation. Its speakers' traditional homeland was Bactria, a region the faith's own scripture lists among the first lands ever created, and its founder is said by later Persian poets to have died within the walls of Bactria's capital, a city Alexander the Great found already ancient when he marched in and took it in 329 BCE without much of a fight. Its scattered oral texts didn't get a proper alphabet until roughly six centuries after Alexander, when a Sasanian king named Shapur II ordered his officials to invent one specifically to freeze the correct pronunciation of prayers that had been drifting slightly with every generation of priests who recited them. Its priests still cover their nose and mouth with a cloth called a padan during fire-tending rituals, not to keep anything secret but to keep their own breath from contaminating a flame considered too sacred to touch with human exhalation, a practice formalized once Zoroastrianism became the Sasanian state religion in 224 CE. Its speakers scattered for good when Arab armies toppled the Sasanian Empire in the 7th century, and the refugees who eventually settled in India built a business dynasty so enduring that one of its descendant families now owns Jaguar Land Rover. It still gets chanted daily by working priests from Mumbai to Yazd, most of whom can recite it with note-perfect precision without necessarily being able to hold a casual conversation in it.

#### eras
---
Avestan changes with whoever's carrying the faith: an Old Avestan/Gathic Era once Zoroaster composes his hymns, a Young Avestan Era once later liturgical texts accumulate, an Achaemenid Era once the Persian Empire adopts Zoroastrian-adjacent practice, a Sasanian Compilation Era once the Avesta is written down and the faith becomes state religion, and a Modern Parsi Diaspora Era once refugees carry the tradition to India.
