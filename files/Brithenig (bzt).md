Brithenig exists to answer a single what-if question: what would a Romance language sound like if it had evolved in Britain instead of on the European mainland? Andrew Smith, a language hobbyist from Dunedin, New Zealand, built Brithenig in 1996 as a thought experiment, imagining a Britain where Vulgar Latin displaced the native Brythonic Celtic languages rather than dying out after Rome's withdrawal; the result is an artificial sister language to French, Catalan, Spanish, Portuguese, Romanian, Occitan, and Italian, but one whose sound changes mimic those that shaped Welsh, and whose vocabulary borrows from the Brittonic languages and English throughout its invented history. Other conlang hobbyists soon discovered Smith's project online, and by late 1997 and early 1998 it had grown into a full communal effort, the Ill Bethisad alternate-history setting, built collectively to give Brithenig and its sibling Romance conlangs a shared fictional world to inhabit. Smith's work earned wider recognition in 2008, when he was featured in "Esperanto, Elvish, and Beyond: The World of Constructed Languages," an exhibit at the Cleveland Public Library running from May through August of that year. By March 2021, the Ill Bethisad project counted 58 active participants collaborating on its languages and shared timeline. Because Brithenig has no gestural tradition of its own, and its creator is from New Zealand, it borrows a thematic analog from that country's deaf-rights history: the New Zealand Sign Language Act, which received royal assent on April 10, 2006.
