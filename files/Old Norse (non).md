Old Norse is the language of a settler who, according to the Icelandic Book of Settlements, threw two carved wooden pillars overboard from his ship and vowed to build his farm wherever they washed ashore, landing in 874 CE at a smoking bay he named Reykjavík. Its longest surviving runic inscription, carved onto a five-ton granite slab in Sweden around 800 CE, runs to roughly 760 characters of riddling verse that scholars still argue over the meaning of. Its speakers built their most enduring political institution not around a king but around an open-air assembly at Þingvellir, founded in 930 CE, where free farmers gathered annually to hear the law recited aloud by a memory-trained Lawspeaker. Its legal tradition took non-verbal communication seriously enough that Norway's Frostaþing Lawbook specified exactly how a man whose tongue had been cut out could still identify his attacker, through carved runes or pointed gestures, and at least one Icelandic saga preserves a real sibling relationship built entirely on that principle, with a non-speaking woman named Oddný answering her brother by cutting runes into a wooden rod. Its own era as a living tongue closed out violently, ending symbolically in 1066 CE when the last great Viking army was cut down at Stamford Bridge nineteen days before its English victors had to turn around and fight again at Hastings. It still gets studied through the sagas, still gets mined for fantasy-novel vocabulary, and still gets spoken, more or less, by modern Icelanders who can read 800-year-old manuscripts without much trouble.

#### eras
---
Old Norse changes with whoever's raiding or settling: a Proto-Norse/Elder Futhark era predating the Viking Age proper, a Viking Age era once raids on the British Isles begin, an Icelandic Commonwealth/Saga Age era once the Althing is founded, a Christianization era once Scandinavia formally converts, and a Norwegian Crown era once Iceland submits to external rule.
