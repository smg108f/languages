Old English is the language of a herdsman at a Yorkshire monastery who, according to the monk Bede, had never composed a verse in his life until a figure in a dream ordered him to sing, producing sometime between 658 and 680 CE the nine lines now recognized as the oldest securely dated poem in the language. Its most important seat of power wasn't chosen for battlements or rivers but inherited an ecclesiastical prestige in 676 CE, when the Bishop of Wessex moved his seat there and made its Old Minster a cathedral two centuries before a king named Alfred would rule from the same city and personally commission the vernacular prose that helped standardize the language. Its speakers spent the better part of a century fighting off Viking raiders who first sacked a Northumbrian monastery in 793 CE, a struggle Alfred only reversed at the Battle of Edington in 878, buying Old English another two centuries of life before a Norman duke's cross-Channel invasion in 1066 began quietly replacing it with French-inflected Middle English. Monks at Canterbury, forbidden from speaking under the Benedictine Rule, had by around 1000 CE written down a list of 127 hand signs covering everything from foods to visiting bishops, so daily monastery business could carry on in total silence. It still gets read in university seminars through an epic about a monster-killing warrior whose manuscript nearly burned in a London library fire, and its grammar still trips up anyone who assumed English always worked the way it does now.
