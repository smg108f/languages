Akkadian is the language of a usurper king who claimed to have been set adrift as a baby in a reed basket on the Euphrates, only to grow up and found a capital city named Akkad around 2334 BCE, adapting his rivals' Sumerian cuneiform script to write down his own Semitic tongue for the first time in the process. Its speakers spent the next two thousand years building and losing empires, watching Gutian raiders topple Sargon's dynasty within two centuries, before a Babylonian king named Hammurabi issued a law code around 1754 BCE that stands among the earliest surviving legal texts anywhere to address deafness directly. Its diplomatic prestige peaked in the 14th century BCE, when Egyptian pharaohs and Near Eastern kings who agreed on almost nothing else all corresponded in Akkadian cuneiform, the era's default language of international business. Its greatest library, assembled at Nineveh under an Assyrian king who wanted to own a copy of everything ever written, held some 30,000 clay tablets before it burned in 612 BCE, a fire that ironically baked the clay hard enough to preserve most of them for archaeologists to dig up three thousand years later. Its position as the region's working language finally ended in 745 BCE, when an Assyrian king replaced it with Aramaic for imperial paperwork simply because Aramaic's alphabet was faster to write than cuneiform, though scribes and priests kept copying Akkadian texts in shrinking numbers for another eight centuries after that. It still gets read off clay tablets in museum basements, and its last known dated inscription, an astronomical almanac, was pressed into clay in 75 CE, nearly two thousand years after most people had stopped speaking it as a first language.

#### eras
---
Akkadian changes with whoever's ruling Mesopotamia: an Old Akkadian Era once Sargon founds his empire, an Old Babylonian/Old Assyrian Era once Hammurabi codifies law from Babylon, an Amarna Age once Akkadian becomes the Near East's diplomatic lingua franca, a Neo-Assyrian Imperial Era once Assyria dominates the region, and an Aramaic Ascendancy Era once Akkadian is retired from official use but survives in scribal and scholarly hands.
