Yiddish is the language of a people who never had a state to name a capital of, so its speakers picked a city 900 kilometers from where the language was born and simply started calling it "Jerusalem." The oldest surviving fragment of it is a two-line rhyming couplet a scribe jotted into the margin of a Hebrew prayer book in 1272, almost seven centuries before anyone got around to founding an institute to standardize its spelling. It once had roughly 13 million speakers stretched across Central and Eastern Europe, a number the Holocaust cut by more than half within six years, destroying what its own survivors called, in the same word used for the fall of the Temple in Jerusalem, a Khurbn. It didn't get a proper school for its Deaf community until 1911, in a Polish industrial city, and even then a similarly Yiddish-steeped school two hundred kilometers away in Vilna somehow ended up teaching in Russian instead. Its speakers today are disproportionately Hasidic families raising more children per household than almost anywhere else in the developed world, which means a language pronounced dead by demographers in the 20th century is, numerically, doing something closer to the opposite.

#### eras
---
Yiddish changes with wherever its speakers have been permitted to settle: an ancient Rhineland-settlement era predating the language itself, an early Rhineland Ashkenazi era once the fusion language emerges, a Vilna "Jerusalem of Lithuania" golden-age era, an Eastern European Yiddishland era of mass emigration following the 1880s pogroms, and a post-Holocaust displacement era.
