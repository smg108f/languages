Klingon exists because a movie needed its aliens to sound like more than grunts. For Star Trek III: The Search for Spock, released in 1984, Paramount Pictures brought in linguist Marc Okrand to build a real, speakable language out of a handful of Klingon names and a few improvised lines from an earlier film; rather than base it on any existing human language, Okrand drew on his own training in how languages work to construct something deliberately alien in its sound and grammar. Okrand had no idea the language would outlive the production, but fan interest afterward convinced him to write it all down, and The Klingon Dictionary, published in 1985, went on to sell more than 300,000 copies and gave hobbyists worldwide an actual grammar to learn from. In 1992, psychology researcher and Star Trek fan Lawrence M. Schoen founded the Klingon Language Institute in Flourtown, Pennsylvania, to bring together the language's growing scholarly and amateur community; today it counts roughly 2,500 members across more than 50 countries. Klingon's cultural staying power was tested most directly in 2016, when the nonprofit Language Creation Society, born out of a UC Berkeley student group, filed a legal brief partly written in Klingon arguing that the language itself could not be copyrighted, during Paramount and CBS's lawsuit against the fan film Axanar. Marc Okrand's own career gives Klingon an unusually direct signed-form analog: from the day the National Captioning Institute opened in 1979 until roughly 2013, Okrand worked there as its first supervisor of captioning, helping develop real-time closed captioning for deaf and hard-of-hearing television viewers, a technology that debuted publicly at the 1982 Academy Awards broadcast.
