ROILA was never meant for people to speak to each other; it was built so people could be reliably understood by machines. Omar Mubin, a PhD researcher in the Department of Industrial Design at Eindhoven University of Technology, introduced the RObot Interaction LAnguage to the CHI 2009 conference in Boston as a proposed fix for a stubborn problem: general-purpose speech recognition software of the era struggled badly with natural human languages once real-world noise and accents entered the picture. Rather than optimize a robot to understand English better, Mubin's team optimized a language for the robot instead, running a genetic algorithm over generations of candidate vocabularies to select words built from easily distinguished, low-confusion phonemes, favoring simple consonant-vowel syllables that both humans and machines could parse reliably. Follow-up studies conducted with Christoph Bartneck at the University of Canterbury in New Zealand and Loe Feijs back in Eindhoven tested the resulting vocabulary against English using word-spotting experiments and LEGO Mindstorms robots, refining the design through 2010. Mubin defended his completed thesis, "ROILA – RObot Interaction LAnguage," at Eindhoven on June 1, 2011, and continued extending the project's applied research afterward, including a 2013 paper written with Bartneck once Mubin had relocated to the University of Western Sydney. The language and its tools were released under a Creative Commons license and remain available as an open-source project. Because ROILA was engineered purely for machine intelligibility and has no gestural tradition of its own, it borrows one from its country of origin: the Reverend Henri Daniel Guyot's founding of the first Dutch institute for deaf education, in Groningen on April 14, 1790.
