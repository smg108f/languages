Damin was not built for a book, a film, or an international auxiliary movement; it was built, according to Lardil and Yangkaal tradition, by an ancestral figure in the Dreaming, and it was spoken only by fully initiated men of Mornington Island and the surrounding Wellesley Islands in the Gulf of Carpentaria, Queensland, Australia. Structurally, Damin functioned as what linguists call an "anti-language": it borrowed Lardil's grammar almost wholly intact while replacing its everyday vocabulary with a deliberately small, esoteric lexicon of no more than about 150 morphemes, learned as part of an advanced stage of male initiation. Its most striking feature is phonological: Damin used ejective and click consonants at multiple places of articulation, sounds unattested elsewhere in Australian languages and, outside of Africa, essentially unattested anywhere else in the world. The language's transmission was already under severe strain by the time outside scholars arrived; the Presbyterian mission established at Gununa on Mornington Island in May 1914 restricted the initiation ceremonies through which Damin was traditionally taught, and by 1967 the language was no longer in active use. Linguist Kenneth Hale, working from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, carried out landmark fieldwork among Lardil speakers beginning in the early 1960s and conducted his most detailed recordings in December 1973, producing the primary scholarly record of a language whose last fully competent speakers died in the 1980s. Damin's gestural dimension was not borrowed from elsewhere: first-order Lardil male initiates traditionally used a genuine hand-sign system called Marlda Kangka ("hand language"), documented alongside Damin itself and shown by later research to classify the natural world somewhat differently than spoken Lardil does.
