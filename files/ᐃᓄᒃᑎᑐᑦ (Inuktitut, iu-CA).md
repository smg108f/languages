Inuktitut is the language whose writing system arrived by accident of geography, adapted in the 1850s by Anglican missionaries who had already built a syllabary for Cree and simply pointed it north, first printed as scripture verses at a Hudson's Bay Company post before most Inuktitut speakers had ever seen their own language on a page. Its territorial capital didn't exist as a permanent settlement until 1942, when an Inuit guide helped American engineers pick a gravel strip wide enough for wartime aircraft, and the tent camp that grew up around the airstrip wasn't named Nunavut's capital until a 1995 vote made it official four years before the territory itself existed. Its 20th century included a 1918 influenza epidemic that killed roughly a third of the Inuit population of Labrador in a matter of weeks, and a 1953 government relocation that shipped over 90 people 2,000 kilometres north to assert Arctic sovereignty, promising a return trip that never came. Its Deaf community has quietly kept its own indigenous sign language alive since at least 1930, entirely separate from the American Sign Language now taught in southern boarding schools. It still eats whale skin and blubber as a delicacy, throat-sings in overlapping duets that end when one singer breaks into laughter, and made the first feature film ever shot entirely in the language a Cannes prizewinner in 2001.

#### eras
---
Inuktitut changes with whoever's asserting sovereignty over the ice: an ancient Thule/Inuit Settlement era predating European contact, a Missionary Contact/Syllabics era once Anglican missionaries arrive, a Frobisher Bay/Modern Settlement era beginning with the wartime airstrip, a High Arctic Relocation era of Cold War sovereignty claims, and a post-1999 Nunavut Territory era.
