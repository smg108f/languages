Solresol is what happens when a violinist decides an international auxiliary language should also be playable as music. Jean-François Sudre, born in Albi, France, on August 15, 1787, and trained at the Conservatoire de Paris under François Habeneck and Charles Simon Catel, devised the language in 1827, building its entire vocabulary from just seven syllables drawn from the musical solfège scale: do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, si. From the start Sudre designed Solresol to be genuinely multi-modal, expressible not only through speech and singing but through musical instruments, whistling, numbers, colors, flags, and, crucially, hand gestures intended specifically for people with hearing disabilities; he trained performers Édouard Deldevez and Charles Larsonneur to play and interpret the system on violin, and the trio toured France demonstrating it to audiences. Sudre's invention won prizes at international exhibitions in both Paris and London, including recognition at the 1855 Paris Exposition Universelle. He died on October 3, 1862, before completing his definitive account of the system; his widow saw Langue Musicale Universelle published posthumously in 1866, giving Solresol its first full written exposition. The language reached its peak of organized popularity when Boleslas Gajewski published Grammaire du Solresol in 1902, and it has enjoyed occasional revivals of online interest since, including a wave of renewed attention following blog and media coverage around 2011.
