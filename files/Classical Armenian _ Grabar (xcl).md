Classical Armenian, known to its own speakers as Grabar or "written language," is the liturgical register of Armenian that exists because a fifth-century monk got tired of borrowing other people's alphabets. Its story really starts in 301 CE, when King Tiridates III converted to Christianity at his royal residence in Vagharshapat, making Armenia the first state in history to adopt Christianity officially, decades before Rome bothered. Its writing system didn't exist yet at that point: Armenians were still using Greek, Syriac, or Persian script for anything that needed recording, until the monk Mesrop Mashtots designed a dedicated 36-letter alphabet in 405 CE specifically so the Bible could finally be translated into a language ordinary Armenians could read. Its church tightened its own house rules not long after, when the Council of Shahapivan convened in 444 CE and issued twenty canons regulating clergy and monastic conduct, formalizing exactly the kind of bowing, prostration, and sign-of-the-cross gesture still central to Armenian liturgy today. Its independence got tested badly in 451 CE, when the general Vardan Mamikonian led Armenian forces against the Sasanian Empire at the Battle of Avarayr and lost on the battlefield anyway, a defeat so strategically pointless for Persia that it led directly to the 484 CE Nvarsak Treaty guaranteeing Armenians the right to keep practicing Christianity. Its literary output kept accumulating for over a thousand years after that, until a printer named Hakob Meghapart, working out of Venice in 1512 because printing presses back home weren't an option, produced the first printed Armenian book, a prayer collection called the Urbatagirk. Its speakers suffered catastrophically in 1915, when the Armenian Genocide killed somewhere between 664,000 and 1.2 million Armenians and scattered the survivors into diaspora communities from Glendale to Beirut, where Grabar now survives almost exclusively as the language chanted in the Armenian Apostolic Church's liturgy.

#### eras
---
Classical Armenian changes with whoever's converting kings or printing books: an Arsacid Christianization Era once Tiridates III converts at Vagharshapat, an Alphabet/Golden Age Era once Mashtots invents the script and Bible translation begins, a Religious Autonomy Era once the Nvarsak Treaty follows the Battle of Avarayr, a Print Era once the first Armenian book comes off a Venetian press, and a Modern Genocide-Diaspora Era once the Armenian Genocide scatters its speakers worldwide.
