Pennsylvania Dutch is the language of thirteen families from Krefeld who landed in Philadelphia in October 1683 and founded, six miles up an old Indian trail, the first permanent German-speaking settlement in the American colonies, decades before the Palatine migrations that would actually give the dialect its lasting speaker base. Its oldest known poem wasn't written down until 1849, printed in a church newspaper by people whose grandparents had been speaking the dialect at home for over a century without bothering to put it on paper. Its 20th century included two world wars against Germany that made its speakers so uncomfortable with their own accents that most stopped teaching the language to their children entirely, leaving it to survive almost nowhere outside Amish and Old Order Mennonite households that never had televisions to unlearn it from. It didn't get a dedicated regional school for its Deaf community until 1820, the third such school built anywhere in the country. It still marks the first Sunday after Candlemas by watching whether a groundhog can see its own shadow, plays a card game invented in 1937 specifically to teach Amish children their colors, and considers a chocolate empire built by a Mennonite candy-maker's apprentice from Lancaster County one of its proudest exports.

#### eras
---
Pennsylvania Dutch changes with whoever's welcoming German speakers ashore: an ancient Palatine German-speaking era predating emigration, a Germantown/Colonial Settlement era beginning with the 1683 landing, a Palatine Migration/Growth era once the main 18th-century wave arrives, an Anti-German World War era of wartime suppression, and a post-1945 Amish/Mennonite Preservation era.
