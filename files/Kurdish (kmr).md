Kurdish is the language of a people whose closest thing to a stateless nation's biography is a 2,650-couplet 17th-century poem about two doomed lovers, written specifically because its author was tired of watching Kurdish get treated as unfit for serious literature. Its Latin alphabet is younger than its speakers' grandparents, designed in 1932 by an exiled Ottoman prince publishing a magazine out of Damascus because the empire that used to rule his people had just collapsed and nobody else was going to standardize it for him. Its most fortified city changed hands so many times across three millennia of Mesopotamian empires that a Kurdish dynasty finally got to rule from behind its basalt walls for exactly a century in the 11th century before losing it again. Its 20th century included a chemical weapons attack that killed thousands of civilians in a single afternoon and a military campaign the following months that killed well over a hundred thousand more, atrocities its speakers now mark every spring with bonfires and a folk dance instead of mourning alone. It didn't get its own school for the Deaf until 1982, and even then nobody's sure whose sign language the first students actually used. It stuffs vegetables with rice and meat, drinks tea strong enough to stain the glass, and considers a quiet two-player board game just as central to a spring picnic as the bonfire itself.

#### eras
---
Kurdish changes with whoever's controlling the Tigris-Euphrates highlands: an ancient Corduene/Kurdish highland-kingdoms era predating Islam, a Marwanid Kurdish principality era after the Seljuks' rise, an Ottoman-Safavid Kurdish emirates era, a post-WWI partition era following the region's division among four new nation-states, and a post-1991 Kurdistan Autonomous Region era.
