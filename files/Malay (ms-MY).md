Malay is the language of a river-mouth trading post so well positioned on a narrow strait that a fleeing exiled prince turned it into an empire within decades, and whose fastest-growing 15th-century port then spent the next five centuries getting fought over by the Portuguese, the Dutch, and the British in that exact order. It carved its earliest known inscription onto stone in the 7th century under an empire that ruled the sea lanes rather than the land, later adopted an Arabic-based script alongside Islam, and only settled its now-dominant Latin spelling in the 20th century under two separate colonial administrations that couldn't quite agree on how to spell it. It still eats a coconut-rice breakfast dish so ubiquitous it's treated as a national identity marker, produces a beloved mid-century film icon who directed himself into more than 60 movies while also writing some 360 songs, and endured, in living memory, both a brutal wartime occupation and a decade-plus communist insurgency fought largely through the country's own rubber plantations and tin mines.

#### eras
---
Malay changes with whoever's controlling the Strait of Malacca: an Old Malay era shared as Srivijaya's trade lingua franca, a Classical Malay era launched when an exiled prince founded Malacca and turned Islamic learning and commerce into a single enterprise, a colonial era of successive Portuguese, Dutch, and British control, a Japanese Occupation era during WWII, and a post-independence Malaysia era of nation-building and standardized national language policy.
