Malagasy is the language of an island whose ancestors sailed roughly 5,000 kilometers of open ocean from Borneo in outrigger canoes long before anyone that far away had business showing up off Africa's coast, and whose closest linguistic relative today is still spoken by a small community in southern Borneo who have never seen a lemur. Its oldest surviving manuscripts were written not in the language's own Latin alphabet but in a modified Arabic script kept as a sacred treasury by aristocratic scribes on the southeast coast, before a 19th-century king scrapped it for the missionaries' Latin letters by royal decree in 1823. It fought a war against French annexation, lost its monarchy to it in 1896, then rose up again half a century later in an uprising so brutally suppressed that French troops reportedly threw living prisoners out of airplanes. It reburies and redresses its own ancestors' corpses in a dance-accompanied ceremony every few years, plays a strategy board game older than the country's written alphabet, and still supplies most of the world's vanilla from smallholder farmers who rarely see much of the money it makes downstream.

#### eras
---
Malagasy changes with whoever's controlling the central highlands: an ancient Austronesian settlement era launched by seafarers from Borneo, a Sultanates/coastal-kingdoms era of scattered regional rule, a Merina Kingdom era launched by Antananarivo's founding, a French colonial era beginning with the fall of the Merina monarchy, and a post-1960 independent Republic era.
