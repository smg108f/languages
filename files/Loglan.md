Loglan is the constructed language built not to help people talk to each other but to test whether the language you speak actually shapes how you think. American sociologist James Cooke Brown began developing it in 1955 specifically as an experimental tool for probing the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, the idea that a language's grammar and vocabulary constrain what its speakers can conceive of, and he designed Loglan to be as logically unambiguous, culturally neutral, and structurally unlike any natural language as he could manage. The project went public in 1960, when Scientific American ran an article introducing the language to a much wider readership than academic linguistics circles alone, and interest snowballed enough that Brown founded The Loglan Institute in Gainesville, Florida, in 1975 to formalize the research and keep developing the grammar and vocabulary. The institute's tight control over the language eventually backfired: in 1987, after disputes over Brown's trademark and copyright claims on "Loglan" itself, a group of enthusiasts led by Bob LeChevalier split off entirely, forming the Logical Language Group and building their own version from Loglan's published grammar, which they renamed Lojban specifically so it could be developed and shared without the original institute's restrictions. Brown continued directing The Loglan Institute until his death on February 13, 2000, by which point his once-singular linguistic experiment had effectively forked into two separate, competing logical languages carrying on in parallel. Because Loglan itself has no dedicated gestural tradition, it borrows one from its country of origin: Gallaudet University, chartered by Abraham Lincoln in 1864, stands as the first institution of higher education in the world built specifically for deaf students.
