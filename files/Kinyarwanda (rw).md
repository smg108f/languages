Kinyarwanda is the language of a kingdom ruled by a sacred king whose royal court finally settled on a permanent capital only in 1899, after centuries of a monarchy that had coalesced gradually since the 15th century without ever quite picking one hill to call home. Its first substantial written form arrived not from its own scribes but from European missionaries, who had the Four Gospels translated into it by 1904, decades before the rest of the Bible followed in 1957. It endured a revolution in 1959 that killed thousands and sent hundreds of thousands into exile, and then, three decades later, suffered a genocide over roughly 100 days in 1994 that killed as many as a million of its own people, a horror the country now marks every April while asking its citizens to spend one Saturday a month doing collective manual labor as a matter of law. It ferments its bananas into beer for weddings and harvest festivals, plays a mancala-style board game so old that stone-carved boards are attributed to a 16th-century king, and produced, seven years after the genocide, the first feature film ever shot inside its own borders.

#### eras
---
Kinyarwanda changes with whoever's controlling the central highlands: an ancient Kingdom of Rwanda era coalescing gradually from the 15th century, a German colonial era after Rwanda's incorporation into German East Africa, a Belgian colonial/mandate era following the First World War, a post-1962 independent Republic era, and a post-1994 Fourth Republic era following the genocide.

#### signed
---
signed-name :: Rwandan Sign Language (RSL)

global-signers :: no comprehensive national count published; an estimated 10,000 deaf children in Rwanda, of whom only a small fraction are enrolled in school

signer-locations :: Rwanda (Kigali, Butare/Huye, Nyabihu)

governing-bodies ::
traces to Father Joseph Fraipont Ndagijimana's 1962 center for persons with disabilities in Gatagara, Rwanda's earliest documented institution serving disabled children including the Deaf; dedicated deaf schools such as those in Nyange (2008) and Nyabihu (2013) followed; not officially recognized as a national language
