Elamite is the language isolate of one of the ancient Near East's most persistent rivals to Mesopotamia, spoken across what's now southwestern Iran for roughly 2,800 uninterrupted years without ever once bothering to be related to anything else on Earth. Its earliest writing, the still-undeciphered Proto-Elamite script, appears at Susa around 3100 BCE, making Elam's capital one of the oldest cities to develop writing at all, though scholars have made far more progress with the Elamite cuneiform that scribes borrowed from Akkadian afterward: the earliest surviving Elamite cuneiform text is a treaty between Akkadian king Naram-Sin and the Elamite ruler Khita, inscribed around 2250 BCE. Political power consolidated at Susa around 2000 BCE, turning the city into Elam's enduring capital and, eventually, one of the great administrative centers of the Achaemenid Persian Empire that came after it. Elamite religious art left behind an equally durable gesture: a newly excavated rock relief from Izeh, Khuzestan, dated to roughly 1975 BCE, shows an Elamite king seated with his right hand raised in prayer toward a solar disc representing Nahhunte, the sun and justice god, a wordless devotional posture that appears again and again across Elamite religious iconography. Elam's long rivalry with Mesopotamia came to a violent head in 647 BCE, when the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal sacked Susa, demolished its ziggurat and walls, and deported its people and divine images to Nineveh, a blow so severe that later Assyrian scribes boasted of salting the earth, though archaeology shows Elamite culture limped on regardless. The language survived long enough to serve as one of three official languages of the Achaemenid Empire, carved alongside Old Persian and Babylonian on the Behistun Inscription, before finally dying out under Hellenistic rule. Susa itself went quiet for two millennia until French archaeologist Jacques de Morgan began systematic excavations there in 1897, giving Elamite studies their modern start.
