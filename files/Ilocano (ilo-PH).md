Ilocano is the language of a coastal trading network so well-established that Chinese, Japanese, and Malay merchants were already doing business there when a Spanish captain sailed into its harbor in 1572 and simply renamed the town after his own patron. Its first printed book, a 1621 catechism translated by an Augustinian friar working alongside a blind local scholar considered the father of its literature, doubled as an accidental grammar lesson, since nobody had bothered to write the language down for Europeans before. Its farmers rose up in 1807 over a colonial wine monopoly that made it illegal to distill their own rice liquor at home, a rebellion the Spanish crushed by beheading its leaders. Its most famous political export ran the country for two decades, married a woman who kept thousands of pairs of shoes, and left behind a fortune international courts still can't agree on the size of, somewhere between several hundred million and ten billion dollars. It ferments sugarcane into a wine strong enough to fuel an uprising, cures its pork belly into a crackling-skinned specialty, and still tells the story of a folk hero who fought monsters with a talking rooster and a dog long before anyone wrote any of it down.

#### eras
---
Ilocano changes with whoever's controlling the coast: an ancient pre-colonial trading-port era serving Chinese, Japanese, and Malay merchants, a Spanish colonial era following Vigan's founding, an American colonial era after the 1898 cession, a post-1946 independent Republic era, and a post-1986 democratic era following the People Power Revolution.
