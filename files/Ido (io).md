Ido is the constructed language born directly out of Esperanto's own success, when a committee tasked with picking the world's best international auxiliary language decided Esperanto was almost right and then quietly rewrote it instead. The Delegation for the Adoption of an International Auxiliary Language, founded in 1901 by French academics Louis Couturat and Léopold Leau after watching international conferences drown in translation chaos at the 1900 Paris World's Fair, convened a formal committee in Paris in October 1907 to settle the matter once and for all. Among the entries considered was an anonymous proposal signed only "Ido," Esperanto for "offspring," submitted after the official deadline, and the committee ultimately endorsed Esperanto in principle but recommended exactly the reforms that anonymous submission had proposed, effectively birthing a new language on the spot. Ido's backers organized quickly: the Uniono por la Linguo Internaciona Ido, its central governing organization, was formally established in Amsterdam on July 31, 1910, giving the reform movement an institutional home in the Netherlands. Momentum collapsed almost as fast as it built. Louis Couturat, Ido's most tireless advocate, died in an automobile accident on August 3, 1914, just as the outbreak of the First World War scattered the international conference circuit that had sustained the movement, and by 1928 the defection of prominent Danish linguist Otto Jespersen, who published his own rival planned language Novial instead, seemed to seal Ido's fate as a historical footnote. Ido never vanished completely, limping along through small dedicated circles until the internet gave it, like so many niche constructed languages, an unexpected second wind among online enthusiasts.
