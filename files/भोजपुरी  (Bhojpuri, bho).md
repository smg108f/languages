Bhojpuri is the language of a cultural belt so committed to exporting its own people that hundreds of thousands of its speakers left as indentured laborers within a single century, planting the language permanently onto Mauritius, Fiji, Trinidad, Guyana, and Suriname long before anyone coined the word "diaspora" for it. It has no state named after it, no single official capital, and was for generations written in a merchant's shorthand script that quietly vanished from official use within a few decades, yet it built a home-grown folk theatre tradition so influential that its creator is still called the "Shakespeare of Bhojpuri," and a film industry nicknamed "Bhojjiwood" worth hundreds of crores. It considers a four-day sun-worship festival non-negotiable enough to shut down entire cities' worth of riverbanks, treats a mashed-eggplant-and-roasted-wheat dish as comfort food royalty, and produced an 80-year-old one-armed rebel who kept the British Army busy for the better part of a year during 1857 — then died six days after finally winning.

#### eras
---
Bhojpuri changes with whoever's controlling the middle Gangetic plain: an ancient era of Magadha and Kosala janapadas predating any fixed Bhojpuri identity, a Mughal era during which the region was briefly interrupted by the Bhojpuri-born Sher Shah Suri's own empire, a mid-19th-century indenture era that scattered speakers across four continents as plantation labor, an 1857-rebellion era centered on local armed resistance to British rule, and a post-independence Bhojpuri cinema era that gave the language its first mass popular-culture export.
