Latin is the language of a city whose own founders couldn't agree on when it started, settling by tradition on 753 BCE for a settlement that archaeology has since shown really was fortified around roughly that time, whether or not a man named Romulus actually plowed the boundary furrow himself. Its oldest surviving inscription is etched into a gold cloak-pin whose authenticity scholars have spent over a century arguing about, and its second-oldest, found on a small ritual vessel dug out of a Roman gutter in 1880, isn't much less contested. Its speakers conquered enough of Gaul that Plutarch and Appian both claimed a million Celts died and another million were enslaved in the process, figures modern historians treat as wartime propaganda but haven't been able to shrink by much. Its empire fell in 476 CE, but the language didn't: monks at a Burgundian abbey were already, by 1075, writing down over a hundred hand signs so they could run a monastery without breaking their vow of silence, and the Catholic Church made Latin its official legal language by codified statute in 1917, four and a half centuries after the language had stopped being anyone's mother tongue. It still gets recited in Gregorian chant, still gets diagnosed on prescription labels, and still gets used, every conclave, to elect a pope who has to speak not one word of it to get the job.

#### eras
---
Latin changes with whoever's keeping it alive: an Old Latin era beginning with Rome's traditional founding, a Classical Latin era once its literary Golden Age begins, a Medieval Latin era once the Western Roman Empire falls and Latin outlives it, a Renaissance Neo-Latin era once humanists revive classical style, and a Modern Ecclesiastical Latin era once the Catholic Church codifies it as official Church law.
