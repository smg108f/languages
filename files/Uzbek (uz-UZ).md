Uzbek is the language of a Silk Road crossroads so thoroughly fought over that its most famous conqueror built his capital in the very city his own ancestors had been sacked in generations earlier, and whose 15th-century national poet spent his career arguing, in writing, that his native tongue was simply better suited to poetry than the more prestigious literary Persian everyone else was using. It has switched its official alphabet three times in barely a century, from Perso-Arabic to Latin to Cyrillic and partway back to Latin again, largely at the direction of whichever empire happened to be in charge, and only started developing a codified sign language of its own after seven decades of borrowing Russia's. It still eats a saffron-and-carrot rice dish so central to weddings that kazans big enough to feed a thousand guests are considered normal, wrestles competitively in a style claimed to be three thousand years old, and keeps a classical six-part musical suite alive well enough that UNESCO decided the whole world needed to know about it.

#### eras
---
Uzbek changes with whoever's controlling Transoxiana: a Karakhanid era that brought the first written Turkic language to the region, a Chagatai literary era refined under Timur and his successors, a Khanate era once Shaybani Uzbeks took Bukhara and Samarkand, an Imperial Russian/Soviet era of repeated alphabet reform, and a post-1991 independence era still renegotiating script and national identity.

signer-locations :: Uzbekistan (pan-national), centered on Tashkent

governing-bodies ::
Soviet-era deaf schools (established 1940s-1950s in Tashkent and other major cities) taught a Russian Sign Language-based system; since independence, Uzbekistan has worked to develop a distinct UzSL, publishing its first dictionary in 2005 and launching UzSL interpreter degree programs at the National Pedagogical University in 2023
