Tocharian B is the Indo-European language that geography insists shouldn't exist where it was found: attested in the Tarim Basin of what's now western China, thousands of miles east of every other Indo-European branch, and yet grammatically closer to Latin and Celtic than to neighboring Sanskrit or Persian. Its speakers built the Kingdom of Kucha into the largest of the Tarim Basin's Silk Road oasis states around the 2nd century BCE, a wealthy trading center that the Book of Han credits with over 80,000 residents. Its most famous native son was born there in 344 CE: Kumarajiva, son of a Kashmiri father and a Kuchean princess, who grew up to become one of history's most consequential Buddhist translators, rendering a vast body of Sanskrit scripture into Chinese from a translation bureau in Chang'an until his death in 413 CE. Its devotional art flourished alongside him at the Kizil Caves, a complex of over 230 rock-cut Buddhist temples begun around the 3rd century CE and covered in murals whose iconography leans heavily on mudras, the symbolic hand gestures marking moments like the Buddha's victory over Mara. Its manuscripts, the actual evidence for the language itself, mostly date from the 5th century onward, copied in an adapted Brahmi script onto Buddhist texts, monastic records, and caravan-trade documents that sat buried in desert sand for over a thousand years. Its political independence ended in 648 CE, when Tang Dynasty forces under general Ashina She'er took Kucha after a forty-day siege and made it the seat of the Protectorate General to Pacify the West, though Kuchean musicians and their distinctive instruments had already been charming the Tang court for years, eventually shaping court music that traveled as far as Japan. Its manuscripts were rediscovered by German, French, and British expeditions along the Silk Road in the early 20th century, and the language itself, unread by anyone for over a millennium, had to be deciphered essentially from scratch.
