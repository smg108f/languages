Ugaritic is the Northwest Semitic language of a Late Bronze Age port kingdom that mattered enormously for about three centuries and then vanished so completely nobody remembered it existed until a farmer's plow hit a tomb roof in 1928. Its city, Ugarit, sat on the Syrian coast near modern Latakia and reached its political height around 1450 BCE, trading and treaty-making with Egyptians, Hittites, and Mesopotamians alike from a position wealthy enough to leave behind palace archives, private libraries, and a scribal school. Its writing system is the real headline act: sometime around the 14th century BCE, Ugaritic scribes adapted the wedge-shaped cuneiform stylus, previously used for the hundreds of syllabic signs of Akkadian, into a genuinely alphabetic script of just thirty characters, one of the earliest true alphabets anyone has found. Its priests practiced divination the way most of the ancient Near East did, examining sacrificed animal livers for omens, and archaeologists excavating the so-called House of the Hurrian Priest turned up inscribed clay liver models used to train diviners in exactly which marks meant what, with matching ivory models found in the Royal Palace itself. Its end came violently and abruptly: the kingdom was destroyed around 1180 BCE, almost certainly by the Sea Peoples whose raids were reshaping the entire eastern Mediterranean at the time, and one of the last tablets ever inscribed there, an unbaked letter describing enemy ships approaching, was still sitting in its kiln when the city burned, which is why it survived at all. Its rediscovery had to wait 3,000 years, until French archaeologist Claude Schaeffer began excavating Ras Shamra in 1929 and unearthed a library nobody had touched since the Bronze Age collapsed around it, giving scholars an entirely new alphabet to decipher from a language nobody had spoken in three millennia.
