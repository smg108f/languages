Slovio is the pan-Slavic auxiliary language that came first and got overtaken by its own rebellion. Mark Hučko, born in Bratislava on September 15, 1947, emigrated to Canada in 1968 amid the political upheaval surrounding the Prague Spring and relocated to Switzerland in 1984, where he ran a small computer business; in 1999, working as a self-described amateur linguist rather than an academic one, he launched Slovio online, an Esperanto-style schematic language built from vocabulary roots shared across Russian, Polish, Ukrainian, Belarusian, Czech, Slovak, Slovene, and Bulgarian. By June 2005 its dictionary had grown to roughly 29,000 entries, and at its peak the project drew a small, dedicated community of some 10-15 regular users, mostly members of the Slavic diaspora, alongside a wider circle of interested bystanders. Slovio was heavily criticized, notably in Jan van Steenbergen's 2016 essay "The Slovio Myth," for its artificial, un-Slavic grammar and for the radical Slavic-nationalist rhetoric expressed by some of its users; partly in reaction to these problems, a rival group launched the more naturalistic Slovianski project in 2006, which would go on to merge into modern Interslavic. Hučko stopped updating Slovio's website and replying to correspondence around 2010, and the project has been effectively defunct since 2011. Because Slovio has no gestural tradition of its own, and its creator is Slovak by birth, it borrows a thematic analog from that country's deaf history: the Slovak parliament's 1995 Sign Language Act.
