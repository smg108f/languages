Marwari is the language of a desert kingdom founded in the 13th century by a Rathore clan whose most famous ruler abandoned an older fort town to build an entirely new hilltop capital, one so defensible its walls still dominate the skyline six centuries later. It grew out of a medieval literary tradition once shared with Old Gujarati, produced its finest surviving epic as a court poet's 1455 tribute to a Chauhan king's doomed last stand, and later got its everyday business written down not by scholars but by moneylenders, who invented an entire commercial shorthand script just to keep faster ledgers. It ladles ghee onto baked wheat balls and calls the result a national dish, keeps a hereditary caste of Muslim folk musicians employed at Hindu festivals for generations, and somehow turned "good with money" into an entire global business diaspora stereotype that isn't even wrong.

#### eras
---
Marwari changes with whoever's controlling the Thar Desert: an ancient Gurjara-Pratihara era predating a distinct Marwar identity, a Rathore Kingdom of Marwar era launched by Jodhpur's founding, a Mughal-vassalage era of shifting imperial pressure including the Rathore rebellion, a British princely-state era, and a post-1949 Rajasthani statehood era following Marwar's merger into independent India.
