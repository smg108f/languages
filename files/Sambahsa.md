Sambahsa-Mundialect is what happens when an international auxiliary language designer decides Esperanto's Romance bias isn't ambitious enough, and reaches all the way back to reconstructed Proto-Indo-European instead. French linguist Olivier Simon, who had already built an earlier conlang called Angaté in 1992, began developing Sambahsa around 1999, working for roughly eight years before releasing it on the internet in July 2007. Rather than limiting its vocabulary to a handful of Western European languages, Simon deliberately drew Sambahsa's roughly 19,500-word lexicon from Arabic, Chinese, Indonesian, Swahili, and Turkish alongside its Indo-European core, producing a language whose grammar preserves Proto-Indo-European's four cases and its own predictable ablaut system for the past tense. The project has attracted an unusually literary hobbyist following: translators, chiefly Simon himself, have rendered The Little Prince, Hermann Hesse's Demian, Albert Camus's The Stranger, several Robert E. Howard Conan stories, Arthur Conan Doyle's "A Scandal in Bohemia," and H. P. Lovecraft's "The Call of Cthulhu" into Sambahsa, while the Ireland-based press Evertype has formally published Sambahsa editions of Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, Through the Looking-Glass, and The Strange Case of Doctor Jekyll and Mister Hyde. In 2018, a proposal to register Sambahsa for its own ISO 639-3 code, "sph," was submitted to SIL International in Dallas, Texas and ultimately rejected. Because Sambahsa has no gestural tradition of its own, and its creator is French, it borrows a thematic analog from that country's deaf-education history: Charles-Michel de l'Épée's founding of the world's first free public school for the deaf in Paris in 1760.
