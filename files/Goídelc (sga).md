Goídelc, better known as Old Irish, is the earliest fully attested stage of the Goidelic branch of Celtic, and honestly it earns its listing here on a technicality: it's less "constructed" than it is stubbornly, spectacularly reconstructed by generations of philologists piecing it together from stone, scripture, and saga. Its earliest ancestor stage, Primitive Irish, survives only in Ogham inscriptions, mostly terse personal names carved into standing stones starting around the 4th century CE, using an alphabet of straight and slanted notches cut along a stone's edge rather than anything resembling Latin letters. Ireland's political geography in this era never quite settled on one capital, but the Hill of Tara carried the most symbolic weight as the traditional inauguration site of the High Kings, and Christian legend fixed that symbolism permanently in 433 CE, when Saint Patrick lit a defiant Paschal fire on the nearby Hill of Slane in violation of King Lóegaire's decree that no flame could precede the druids' fire at Tara. Old Irish scribes carried the Ogham alphabet's logic somewhere unexpected: medieval grammatical tracts like In Lebor Ogaim, copied into the fourteenth-century Book of Ballymote, describe "hand ogham" and "foot ogham," entire systems for signing the Ogham letters silently on one's fingers or against the shin, a wordless extension of the alphabet that functioned as a kind of literate hand-code centuries before anyone called it that. Viking raiders began arriving on Irish shores in 795 CE, disrupting monastic life across the island for generations and forcing scriptoria like Iona's into flight, though the tradition survived long enough to produce treasures like the Book of Kells around 800 CE. Modern scholarship finally catalogued the whole Ogham corpus systematically when R.A.S. Macalister completed his Corpus Inscriptionum Insularum Celticarum in 1945, giving philologists a single reference for a language that had spent a millennium and a half scattered across standing stones and monastery libraries.
