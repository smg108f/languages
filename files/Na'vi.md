Na'vi exists because a movie studio called a university linguistics department looking for someone to invent an entire alien tongue from scratch. In 2005, James Cameron's production commissioned Paul Frommer, a USC linguist with a Ph.D. from the university, to build the language of the blue-skinned Na'vi people for what would become Avatar; over the next four years Frommer expanded Cameron's rough phonetic sketches into a full language of 20 consonants, seven vowels, and four diphthongs, deliberately shaped to sound unlike any real-world language family. Avatar reached theaters on December 18, 2009, and became a genuine cultural event, hitting $1 billion in worldwide box office within 17 days and eventually earning roughly $2.92 billion, making it the highest-grossing film of all time. Fans built a learning community almost immediately: LearnNavi.org went online within months of the premiere, and by early 2010 members were reverse-engineering Na'vi grammar from the film's few canonical examples, catching Frommer's own attention; by 2012 he was posting new vocabulary directly to the site and expanding the lexicon based on what the growing community needed. Cameron returned to the world in Avatar: The Way of Water, released December 16, 2022, which added further Na'vi vocabulary and pulled in another $2.33 billion worldwide. Because Na'vi itself has no gestural tradition, and Frommer built it at USC in Los Angeles, it borrows a thematic analog from that city's deaf arts history: Deaf West Theatre, founded there in 1991 by Ed Waterstreet and Linda Bove.
