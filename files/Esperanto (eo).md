Esperanto is the international auxiliary language that actually worked, at least compared to every rival attempt before or since, and it owes its existence to a Warsaw ophthalmologist who grew up watching Poles, Russians, Germans, and Jews in his hometown of Białystok fail to understand each other and decided the fix was a language nobody owned. Ludwik Lejzer Zamenhof spent his teens and twenties quietly refining a grammar and vocabulary, and on July 26, 1887, published it in Russian as the Unua Libro ("First Book"), signed under the pseudonym "Doktoro Esperanto," meaning "one who hopes," a name so appealing that people started calling the whole language Esperanto within two years. The movement needed a way to recognize each other on sight, and in 1892 an article in La Esperantisto proposed the verda stelo, a green five-pointed star, as a badge worn by Esperantists specifically so strangers could identify a fellow speaker without saying a word. The whole project got its symbolic capital in August 1905, when 688 Esperantists from 20 countries gathered in Boulogne-sur-Mer, France, for the first World Esperanto Congress, where Zamenhof deliberately resigned formal leadership of the movement so that his own Jewish identity couldn't be weaponized against it. Esperanto's biggest institutional stamp of approval came from UNESCO, which passed the Montevideo Resolution on December 10, 1954, formally endorsing Esperanto as a legitimate international auxiliary language and pledging to track its progress in education and culture. The language kept finding odd footholds afterward, from William Shatner starring in the entirely-Esperanto horror film Incubus in 1966 to billionaire investor George Soros, whose Esperantist father Tivadar raised him speaking it at home, and its most recent growth spurt came in 2015, when Duolingo launched an Esperanto course and introduced the language to millions of casual learners overnight.
