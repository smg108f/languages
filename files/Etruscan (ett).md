Etruscan is the pre-Roman language of central Italy that nobody has ever definitively linked to any other tongue on earth, a genuine isolate whose script is legible letter-by-letter but whose meaning remains only partly cracked despite two centuries of trying. Its earliest surviving text is a small ivory writing tablet found at Marsiliana d'Albegna, its rim carved with a 26-letter abecedarium around 700 BCE, evidently a schoolchild's reference copy of an alphabet borrowed from Greek colonists to the south. Its speakers organized themselves into a loose confederation of twelve city-states, the Dodecapolis, whose representatives met annually at a shared sanctuary called the Fanum Voltumnae, a site so thoroughly lost that historians have been searching for its exact location since at least the 15th century, though Livy's history places the earliest recorded gathering there in 434 BCE. Its political fortunes started collapsing after a Sicilian-Greek fleet wrecked the Etruscan navy at the Battle of Cumae in 474 BCE, and kept collapsing for over four centuries: Rome took the rival city of Veii after a ten-year siege in 396 BCE, and finally absorbed the last independent Etruscan city, Volsinii, in 264 BCE. Its religious specialists, the haruspices, kept practicing liver divination well into the Roman period using training aids like the Liver of Piacenza, a life-sized bronze model sheep's liver inscribed with the names of the gods assigned to each of its regions, cast around 100 BCE. Its last serious scholar was, improbably, a Roman emperor: Claudius, whose wife's family had Etruscan roots, wrote a twenty-volume history of the Etruscans and compiled a dictionary by personally interviewing the last elderly speakers before his death in 54 CE, after which fluent knowledge of the language essentially died with him. Its longest surviving text, ironically, only survives because it was cut into strips and used to wrap an Egyptian mummy, now sitting in a museum in Zagreb as the oldest surviving European book nobody can fully read.

#### eras
---
Etruscan changes with whoever's building tombs or losing wars: a Villanovan/Proto-Etruscan Era once the preceding Iron Age culture transitions toward historical Etruria, an Archaic Flourishing Era once the alphabet is adopted and cities like Tarquinia rise, an Etruscan League/Federal Era once the twelve cities begin meeting at the Fanum Voltumnae, a Roman Conquest Era once Rome starts absorbing individual Etruscan cities, and a Terminal/Assimilation Era once the last independent Etruscan political identity dissolves into Rome.
