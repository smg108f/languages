Quenya began as a private hobby of a young English philologist rather than as any kind of practical project. J.R.R. Tolkien compiled his first substantial word-list for the language, the "Qenya Lexicon," in 1915 at age 23, drawing its sound and structure primarily from Finnish, with further influence from Latin and Greek, developing the vocabulary further while serving in the trenches during the Battle of the Somme. Tolkien kept revising the grammar privately for decades while building an academic career, taking up the Rawlinson and Bosworth Professorship of Anglo-Saxon at Oxford on July 21, 1925, after an earlier post at the University of Leeds; Quenya and its sister-language Sindarin matured alongside his professional work in historical linguistics rather than apart from it. The language finally reached a wide public audience when Tolkien folded its grammar, script, and sample texts into the appendices of The Return of the King, published October 20, 1955, as the concluding volume of The Lord of the Rings. Decades later, when Peter Jackson adapted the trilogy for film, linguist David Salo was hired to expand Tolkien's fragmentary Quenya and Sindarin into fully speakable dialogue for the 2001-2003 productions filmed around Wellington, New Zealand, putting the language into actors' mouths for the first time. Because Quenya itself has no gestural tradition, and Tolkien wrote it in England, it borrows a thematic analog from that country's own deaf history: the British government's formal recognition of British Sign Language on March 18, 2003.
